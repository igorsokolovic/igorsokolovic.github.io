## Following the Formation of a 2DEL, Atom by Atom

### For the General Public

In our [recent publication in *Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59258-4), we showed that two surfaces of the same SrTiO<sub>3</sub> crystal can behave very differently. The TiO<sub>2</sub>-terminated surface is metallic immediately after cleaving, while the SrO-terminated surface initially behaves as a semiconductor. Yet when exposed to intense synchrotron radiation, SrO also develops a two-dimensional electron liquid (2DEL).

**What changes at the surface to make this happen?**

Synchrotron-based photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) allows us to follow the electronic structure as the surface is irradiated. However, it cannot directly show whether individual atoms have moved or disappeared. For that, we need noncontact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM), which operates in a different experimental setup.

So we decided to **recreate the effects of synchrotron irradiation in our own laboratory**, where we could subsequently examine the surface atom by atom.

We exposed freshly cleaved SrTiO<sub>3</sub> surfaces to controlled doses of X-rays and low-energy electrons, then investigated the changes using nc-AFM and scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS).

The results revealed two important steps.

First, **X-ray irradiation changes the electronic environment of the SrO surface**, bringing its conduction band closer to the energy at which electrons can move freely. But this alone is not enough to make the surface metallic.

The crucial additional step involves **oxygen vacancies**, which are created when energetic electrons remove oxygen atoms from the surface. We could directly identify these missing oxygen atoms with nc-AFM. Their positive charge changes the electrostatic potential, allowing mobile electrons from within the crystal to accumulate near the surface.

Interestingly, the TiO<sub>2</sub> termination showed none of these changes. It was already metallic and remained essentially unaffected by the same irradiation.

By combining the electronic information from synchrotron measurements with atomically resolved laboratory experiments, we could explain why the two surfaces respond so differently to irradiation.

What initially appeared to be simply a light-induced change in electronic conductivity turned out to have a clear connection to **individual missing atoms and their influence on the surrounding electrons**.

The full study, [*Duality and degeneracy lifting in two-dimensional electron liquids on SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001)*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59258-4), was published in *Nature Communications* (2025).

### For Scientists

The formation of two-dimensional electron liquids (2DELs) on SrTiO<sub>3</sub> surfaces under synchrotron irradiation has been known for years. However, identifying the microscopic origin of these electronic states is challenging because **ARPES provides information in momentum space, while the atomic-scale modifications responsible for their formation remain largely inaccessible**.

In our [recent *Nature Communications* paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59258-4), we found that truly bulk-terminated SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001) surfaces exhibit markedly different behavior under synchrotron irradiation. The TiO<sub>2</sub> termination is metallic immediately after cleavage, while the initially semiconducting SrO termination gradually develops a 2DEL.

The Fermi surface on TiO<sub>2</sub> becomes well defined within approximately 2 seconds of synchrotron exposure, whereas SrO requires around 30 seconds under our experimental conditions.

To understand this difference, we needed to correlate the evolution of the electronic structure with the appearance of individual atomic defects.

The difficulty is experimental. **Atomically resolved nc-AFM cannot be performed directly at the synchrotron ARPES measurement position in our setup.** We therefore reproduced the relevant irradiation processes independently in the laboratory, using Al Kα X-rays and 47 eV electrons, followed by nc-AFM and scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS).

All experiments were performed on well-defined, freshly cleaved surfaces, with irradiation carried out at 100 K and the samples kept cold during their transfer back to the microscope.

#### Two contributions to the formation of the 2DEL

The as-cleaved SrO termination contains negatively charged Sr vacancies, which produce upward band bending and leave the surface semiconducting.

Upon X-ray irradiation, photoemission induces a surface photovoltaic effect that substantially reduces this upward band bending. STS shows that the conduction-band onset shifts by approximately **1.5 eV towards the Fermi level**. However, the shift saturates before the surface becomes metallic.

Importantly, nc-AFM detects no newly formed oxygen vacancies following X-ray irradiation. Apart from a few features attributed to adsorbates, the atomic structure remains essentially unchanged.

The missing contribution becomes apparent upon irradiation with **47 eV electrons**. These electrons have sufficient energy to induce oxygen desorption through the Knotek–Feibelman mechanism, creating positively charged oxygen vacancies on the SrO termination.

We directly observed these vacancies in atomically resolved nc-AFM images as defects in the oxygen sublattice. Their appearance was accompanied by a further shift of the conduction-band onset towards the Fermi level.

At an oxygen-vacancy concentration of approximately **0.05 monolayers**, STS places the conduction-band onset within 50 meV of the Fermi level. Accounting for tip-induced band bending, this is sufficient, or close to sufficient, for the formation of a 2DEL.

The two irradiation-induced effects therefore complement one another: photoemission reduces the initial upward band bending, while positively charged oxygen vacancies provide the additional electrostatic modification needed to populate the surface electron liquid.

#### Why the TiO<sub>2</sub> termination behaves differently

The same electron irradiation produces no detectable oxygen vacancies on TiO<sub>2</sub>, and its electronic structure remains essentially unchanged.

This is consistent with our earlier findings that the TiO<sub>2</sub> termination is already metallic after cleavage. Its positively charged Sr adatoms promote downward band bending, enabling electrons to occupy states near the surface without the formation of additional oxygen vacancies.

An important consequence is that **oxygen vacancies on SrO do not simply supply all the electrons forming the 2DEL**. Their principal role is to modify the electrostatic potential and make the relevant conduction-band states energetically accessible. The mobile electrons themselves originate predominantly from the bulk of the Nb-doped crystal.

This also helps explain why the two terminations ultimately develop electron liquids with nearly identical carrier densities despite having very different concentrations of charged surface defects.

By recreating the relevant irradiation processes under conditions compatible with atomic-scale microscopy, we could connect the electronic evolution measured by synchrotron ARPES to directly observed atomic defects and local changes in the density of states.

The result provides a microscopic explanation for **why a freshly cleaved SrO termination is semiconducting, how irradiation enables its transition towards metallicity, and why the TiO<sub>2</sub> termination does not require the same mechanism**.

The complete results are presented in [*Duality and degeneracy lifting in two-dimensional electron liquids on SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001)*, *Nature Communications* **16**, 4594 (2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59258-4).
