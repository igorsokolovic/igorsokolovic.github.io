## Taking Responsibility for the qPlus Laboratory



When **Prof. Martin Setvín** left TU Wien to take up a professorship in Prague, the responsibility for maintaining our **qPlus laboratory** fell to me.

The laboratory houses a highly sensitive low-temperature scanning probe microscope, capable of resolving individual atoms using scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) and noncontact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM). Keeping such an instrument running reliably requires considerably more than knowing how to operate it.

And our particular setup comes with some unusual challenges.

For starters, the microscope is located on the **fifth floor of a building with a metro line running underneath it**. Not exactly the ideal environment for measurements where even the slightest vibrations can become a problem! Instruments this sensitive are commonly installed in basements, on massive concrete foundations, to minimize vibrations.

Our solution? Among other things, **Prof. Michael Schmid's rather ingenious, almost patented-looking arrangement of bungee cords**, which helps isolate the microscope from vibrations transmitted through the building.

But the real complexity becomes apparent once you look inside the microscope.

The measurement head operates at temperatures close to that of liquid helium, with separate **liquid-helium and liquid-nitrogen cryostats** providing cooling and thermal shielding. Rotatable copper shields protect the sample and the qPlus sensor from thermal radiation coming from the warmer surroundings.

Behind these shields sits a remarkably intricate assembly of wiring, springs, electrical connections, and sensitive components.

Every detail matters. The vibration-isolation springs need to be positioned correctly, the cryogenic preamplifier must reliably detect the tiny qPlus signals, and all components must be securely mounted. Loose cables can introduce mechanical vibrations, while inadequate electrical insulation or shielding can produce unwanted noise.

Working on this instrument requires **patience, careful planning, and a healthy respect for how easily a seemingly insignificant detail can compromise the measurements**.

One particularly memorable troubleshooting exercise involved the **eddy-current damping system** at the bottom of the measurement head.

This system uses magnets to damp unwanted motion of the suspended assembly without introducing direct mechanical contact. Unfortunately, if the magnets become misaligned and touch the stationary housing, they create a mechanical connection that effectively bypasses the vibration isolation.

And that was precisely the problem we encountered.

The microscope was picking up unwanted vibrations, and after investigating the possible causes, we traced the issue to the **misaligned damping magnets**. Correcting their position restored the mechanical isolation and allowed us to continue with stable, atomically resolved measurements.

It was a satisfying problem to solve, but also a good reminder of just how many things need to work together for a single high-quality AFM image to appear on the screen.

Taking responsibility for the qPlus laboratory meant looking after all of this: **maintaining the instrument, troubleshooting problems, and ensuring that it remained available for demanding experiments**. Much of that work happens behind the scenes, takes considerable time, and rarely makes it into scientific publications.

Nevertheless, **it's an important part of experimental physics**, and taking on these responsibilities taught me a great deal about the instrument, its limitations, and the engineering needed to push measurements to the atomic scale.
