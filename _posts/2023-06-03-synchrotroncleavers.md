Traveling to a synchrotron for a five-day beamtime is always a slightly special kind of stress. In our case, the measurements were running essentially **24 hours a day**, and with that kind of schedule there is very little room for improvisation, delays, or failed sample preparation.

So when I traveled to **MAX IV in Lund** for our experiments at the **Bloch beamline**, I did what felt excessive at the time but turned out to be absolutely the right decision: I brought **a small army of SrTiO<sub>3</sub> cleaving devices and their components**.

Preparing good cleaved SrTiO<sub>3</sub> surfaces for these experiments is not exactly trivial. The surfaces have to be good enough for demanding measurements under **UHV conditions**, and when you are at a synchrotron, every hour counts. There is no time to discover on site that a part is missing, damaged, contaminated, or simply not working quite as intended.

That is why I took with me not just a few complete devices, but also **many spare components**. Before each new use in UHV, these metal parts need to be **carefully polished and thoroughly chemically cleaned**, so having a larger number of already prepared pieces ready to go is extremely useful. It saves time, reduces stress, and gives a much better chance that at least one really good sample can be prepared when needed.

As the beamtime progressed, this stockpile of hardware became even more useful. In the second half of the experiment, we wanted to prepare and load **additional contingency samples** into UHV, just in case.

That was the moment when some of the more improvised combinations of leftover parts came into play. A few of the cleaving devices looked exactly as designed. Others were assembled from whatever compatible pieces were still available by that stage.

Those were, naturally, the **Frankenstein cleaving devices**.

Fortunately, the story has a happy ending: **the experiments were a total success**, so bringing all that hardware was more than worth it.

Looking back, the whole scene was slightly funny: a table full of polished metal parts, several generations of cleaving devices, and a handful of strange hybrid constructions waiting for their chance to go into UHV.

But this is often what experimental physics looks like behind the scenes. **Careful preparation, redundancy, and a bit of creative engineering can make all the difference.**

A big thank you to everyone involved, especially the team at the Bloch beamline at MAX IV, as well as my collaborators **Prof. Hugo Dil from EPFL in Lausanne, Fei Guo, and Eduardo Bonini Guedes**.

It was a demanding but very rewarding beamtime, and I am very grateful for the teamwork that made it possible.
