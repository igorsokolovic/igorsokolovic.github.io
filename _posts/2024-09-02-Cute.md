## Seeing a Charge Density Wave Atom by Atom in CuTe

### For the General Public

Materials sometimes behave in rather unexpected ways when cooled down. Instead of maintaining their usual arrangement, electrons can spontaneously organize into periodic patterns, accompanied by small shifts in the positions of the atoms. This phenomenon is known as a **charge density wave (CDW)**.

One particularly interesting example is **copper telluride (CuTe)**, a material whose atomic structure contains chains that give it quasi-one-dimensional properties.

Previous experiments had revealed signatures of a charge density wave in its electronic structure. However, there is something especially satisfying about going beyond these indirect signatures and **actually seeing how the atoms rearrange themselves**.

That was my contribution to our collaborative study, [*Real- and reciprocal space characterization of the three-dimensional charge density wave in quasi-one-dimensional CuTe*](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.110.115112).

Using **noncontact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM)**, I was able to resolve individual tellurium atoms on the freshly cleaved CuTe surface. Rather than following their usual periodic arrangement, the atoms displayed a distinct modulation, repeating every five atomic positions along one direction.

More interestingly, the atoms were not simply displaced in one uniform pattern. We observed regions with different arrangements, including groups of **three and two atoms**, or an apparent sequence of **two, one, and two atoms**.

These patterns provided direct, real-space evidence of the lattice distortion associated with the charge density wave.

Conventional diffraction measurements can reveal that an additional periodicity exists, but they do not directly show the positions of individual atoms. With nc-AFM, we could finally examine the modulation itself.

Our collaborators complemented these observations with photoemission experiments, investigating how the electronic structure changes both within and between the atomic layers. Together, the results demonstrated something rather surprising: **although CuTe has strongly one-dimensional characteristics, its charge density wave is actually three-dimensional**, involving coupled distortions across the crystal.

For me, this was a particularly rewarding example of how atomic-resolution microscopy can provide the missing piece in understanding a material's electronic behavior.

The full study was published in [*Physical Review B* **110**, 115112 (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.110.115112).

### For Scientists

Charge density waves (CDWs) arise from electronic instabilities coupled to periodic lattice distortions. Their formation is especially favorable in low-dimensional materials, where Fermi surface nesting and electron-phonon coupling can stabilize modulated ground states.

Copper telluride (CuTe) is an interesting example. Despite its quasi-one-dimensional electronic structure, previous ARPES experiments had revealed a sizable CDW gap, suggesting a pronounced electronic instability. However, identifying the corresponding atomic displacements and understanding the dimensionality of the ordered state required complementary structural information.

In our collaborative paper, [*Real- and reciprocal space characterization of the three-dimensional charge density wave in quasi-one-dimensional CuTe*](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.110.115112), we combined **atomically resolved nc-AFM, momentum-resolved ARPES, and time-resolved ARPES** to investigate the CDW in both real and reciprocal space.

My contribution focused on **directly resolving the periodic lattice distortion associated with the CDW using nc-AFM**.

Freshly cleaved CuTe crystals were investigated at 5 K under ultrahigh vacuum, using a Cu-terminated tip. The surface Te atoms produce an attractive short-range interaction and appear as dark features in the nc-AFM images, while the underlying Cu atoms contribute predominantly to the long-range interaction.

The resulting atomic-resolution images reveal a clear **5 × 1 superstructure**, with the additional periodicity confined to the direction of the atomic chains.

Although low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) can identify this 5 × 1 periodicity through additional diffraction spots, it does not directly reveal how the individual atoms are displaced within the superstructure. **Our nc-AFM measurements resolve those atomic arrangements in real space.**

Interestingly, larger-area images show two distinct CDW domains. In one, the atomic modulation follows a **3 + 2 arrangement**, while the other exhibits an apparent **2 + 1 + 2 arrangement**.

These differences are particularly revealing. They are consistent with different relative arrangements of the distorted chains in adjacent layers. A 3 + 2 modulation in one layer can combine with an oppositely oriented 2 + 3 modulation in the neighboring layer, producing the apparent 2 + 1 + 2 contrast.

Thus, the real-space measurements provide not only direct evidence of the CDW-associated lattice distortion, but also information about its possible interlayer organization.

Our collaborators investigated the electronic counterpart using photon-energy-dependent ARPES, revealing a periodic variation of the CDW gap along the out-of-plane momentum direction. Time-resolved ARPES additionally identified coherent phonon dynamics associated with the CDW and followed the transient closing of its gap through band renormalization.

Together, these results establish that **the CDW in CuTe is intrinsically three-dimensional, despite the material's strongly quasi-one-dimensional character**.

For me, the particularly rewarding aspect was seeing the atomic distortion directly. While electronic spectroscopy reveals the consequences of charge ordering, nc-AFM allows us to examine the underlying atomic structure and identify the distinct arrangements within the modulated lattice.

The full study was published in [*Physical Review B* **110**, 115112 (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.110.115112).
