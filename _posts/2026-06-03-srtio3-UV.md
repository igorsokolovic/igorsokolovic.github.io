## Following the Formation of a 2DEL, Atom by Atom

### For the General Public

What happens when you shine light on a semiconductor? Usually, the light excites electrons, creating mobile charges that eventually recombine once the illumination stops.

But what if the material could **remember that it had been illuminated, even days later?**

This is exactly what we observed on the surface of strontium titanate (SrTiO<sub>3</sub>).

As discussed in our earlier research, a freshly cleaved SrTiO<sub>3</sub> crystal exposes two distinct surface terminations, SrO and TiO<sub>2</sub>. Although they differ by just one atomic layer, their electronic properties are remarkably different. The TiO<sub>2</sub> termination is metallic, while SrO is semiconducting.

In our new study, [*Photoexcited Hole States at the SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001) Surface Imaged with Noncontact AFM*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07114), we discovered another striking difference between them.

When we illuminated the surface with ultraviolet (UV) light, **the electronic properties of SrO changed dramatically**, while TiO<sub>2</sub> remained essentially unaffected.

Using Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM), a technique that measures differences in the electrical potential between a sharp probe and the sample, we observed a substantial change in the surface work function. This quantity describes the energy required to remove an electron from a material.

The surprising part came when we switched off the UV light. Instead of returning to its original value, **the work function of SrO remained changed for days at cryogenic temperatures**.

Even more interestingly, we could reverse the effect.

By scanning selected regions with a scanning tunneling microscope (STM), we injected electrons into the surface and restored its original electronic state. The change was confined to the regions we had scanned, allowing us to create and erase patterns of different work functions on the same surface.

So where does this unusual memory come from?

When UV light excites SrTiO<sub>3</sub>, it creates electrons and positively charged *holes*, which represent missing electrons. An internal electric field near the SrO surface separates these charges, pushing electrons into the crystal and holes towards its surface.

Some of these holes become trapped near atomic defects, preventing them from recombining with electrons. This is related to the broader physics of **charge localization and polarons**, where electronic charges can become stabilized within a crystal lattice.

Our experiments, supported by density functional theory calculations, indicate that the strontium vacancies naturally present on the SrO termination provide favorable sites for trapping these holes.

What I find particularly fascinating is that a reversible electronic change can persist for days, yet remain confined to remarkably small regions of the surface.

The study is currently undergoing peer review, and the full preprint is freely available on [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07114).




### For Scientists

The separation, localization, and lifetime of photoexcited charges are central to the behavior of semiconductors, particularly in photocatalysis and photoelectronic applications. In ionic materials, excess charges may self-localize through lattice distortions to form polarons or become trapped at structural defects, producing long-lived nonequilibrium states.

In our preprint, [*Photoexcited Hole States at the SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001) Surface Imaged with Noncontact AFM*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07114), we investigate these effects on well-defined, bulk-terminated SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001) surfaces using combined STM, nc-AFM, Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM), and density functional theory (DFT).

Our strain-assisted cleaving method produces large SrO- and TiO<sub>2</sub>-terminated domains with their characteristic populations of approximately 14% Sr vacancies and Sr adatoms, respectively. On the Nb-doped crystals, these defects act as acceptors and donors, creating distinctly different electrostatic environments.

The **SrO termination is semiconducting and exhibits pronounced upward band bending**, while TiO<sub>2</sub> is metallic and has comparatively weak downward band bending.

This difference becomes especially important under illumination.


#### A Surprisingly Persistent Surface Photovoltage

Upon illuminating the surface with a 365 nm UV LED, KPFM reveals a pronounced change in the local contact potential difference (LCPD) of the SrO termination. At 78 K, the LCPD shifts from approximately −0.91 V to −1.81 V during illumination, corresponding to a reduction of about 0.9 eV in the surface work function.

After switching off the UV light, the LCPD stabilizes near −1.72 V rather than returning to its original value.

**This photoinduced potential persists for days at cryogenic temperatures**, as demonstrated by measurements at approximately 5 K and 78 K.

In striking contrast, the TiO<sub>2</sub> termination shows no measurable change in its work function under the same illumination conditions.

The strong upward band bending on SrO provides a natural explanation. It creates an internal electric field in the near-surface region that separates photogenerated electron-hole pairs. Electrons are driven towards the bulk, while holes accumulate near the surface, compensating the initial band bending and producing a persistent surface photovoltage.

#### Reversibly Erasing the Trapped Charge

A particularly useful aspect of this phenomenon is its reversibility.

By scanning the UV-irradiated SrO termination in STM mode with a positive sample bias, we can inject electrons and restore the original local work function. Subsequent nc-AFM imaging clearly distinguishes the discharged regions from the surrounding, still-charged surface.

The work function changes from approximately **3.6 eV before illumination to 2.8 eV after illumination**, and STM scanning restores the initial value within the selected region.

The resulting contrast demonstrates that the photoinduced charge can remain spatially confined. Moreover, the entire process can be repeated through successive UV illumination and electron-injection cycles.

This behavior strongly supports a mechanism based on **reversible charge trapping rather than permanent light-induced modifications of the surface structure**.

#### Why Can the Holes Survive for So Long?

The remarkable lifetime of the photovoltage indicates that the photoexcited holes become localized in states where recombination is strongly suppressed.

Such localization is closely related to polaron physics, although it is important to distinguish self-trapping in a defect-free lattice from trapping at existing atomic defects.

Our DFT calculations favor the latter mechanism. They indicate that holes can localize in oxygen 2p orbitals adjacent to the negatively charged Sr vacancies on the SrO termination, creating energetically favorable trapped states.

Together with the initial electric-field-driven separation of electrons and holes, this provides a microscopic explanation for the long-lived surface photovoltage.

The experiments therefore establish a connection between **surface termination, band bending, photoinduced charge separation, and persistent charge trapping**.

They also show that a well-defined oxide surface can retain a photoinduced electronic state for exceptionally long periods at low temperatures, while allowing that state to be locally erased through electron injection.

These findings form part of our preprint [*Photoexcited Hole States at the SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001) Surface Imaged with Noncontact AFM*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07114), currently undergoing peer review.
