## How One Atomic Layer Changes the Electronic Properties of SrTiO<sub>3</sub>

How much can a single atomic layer change the properties of a material? In strontium titanate (SrTiO<sub>3</sub>), the answer turns out to be quite a lot.

SrTiO<sub>3</sub> is a fascinating material for electronics. Although it is normally an insulator, electrons can accumulate close to its surface, forming a **two-dimensional electron liquid (2DEL)**. These electrons are free to move along the surface but are confined in the perpendicular direction, giving rise to unusual electronic properties with potential applications in future electronic and spintronic devices.

However, despite decades of research, some fundamental questions about these electron liquids have remained unresolved. One important reason is that **the exact atomic structure of the SrTiO<sub>3</sub> surface is often unknown**.

This is where our earlier work comes in. Using our strain-assisted cleaving technique, we can prepare SrTiO<sub>3</sub> surfaces with two well-defined atomic terminations: strontium oxide (SrO) and titanium dioxide (TiO<sub>2</sub>). They belong to the same crystal and differ by just one atomic layer.

In our new publication, [*Duality and degeneracy lifting in two-dimensional electron liquids on SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001)*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59258-4), we combined **noncontact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM)**, which reveals the positions of individual atoms, with **angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES)**, which reveals how electrons behave inside the material.

**What we found was striking.**

The **TiO<sub>2</sub>-terminated surface is already metallic after cleaving**, while the **SrO-terminated surface initially behaves as a semiconductor** and develops its electron liquid only after oxygen vacancies are created.

Even more surprisingly, once both surfaces host electron liquids, their electronic structures are different. ARPES reveals **two distinct rings on the TiO<sub>2</sub> termination but only one on SrO**, even though the electron liquids have nearly the same filling.

The difference is linked to subtle changes in the atomic structure and the possible ordering of electron spins. In other words, changing just the outermost atomic layer can fundamentally alter the behavior of electrons beneath it.

For me, this work brings together several years of research on preparing and understanding SrTiO<sub>3</sub> surfaces. It demonstrates why knowing the precise atomic structure is essential when trying to understand, and ultimately control, the electronic properties of materials.

The full study is freely available in [*Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59258-4).


### For Scientists

Two-dimensional electron liquids (2DELs) on SrTiO<sub>3</sub> have been studied extensively, yet their formation mechanisms and electronic structure have remained subjects of debate. A major difficulty has been the uncertainty surrounding the actual surface termination, particularly when comparing results obtained on differently prepared surfaces.

In our paper, [*Duality and degeneracy lifting in two-dimensional electron liquids on SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001)*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59258-4), we address this problem by combining **microscopic ARPES with atomically resolved nc-AFM on truly bulk-terminated SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001)**.

Our previously developed strain-assisted cleaving technique produces large, atomically flat regions of both SrO and TiO<sub>2</sub> termination. Their atomic structures are well defined, including the characteristic population of approximately 14% Sr vacancies on SrO and 14% Sr adatoms on TiO<sub>2</sub>, associated with compensation of the cleavage-induced polarity.

This allowed us to correlate the atomic structure measured by nc-AFM with the electronic states observed by synchrotron-based ARPES. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provides the crucial link between the two techniques by identifying sufficiently large domains of each termination.

**Despite differing by only one atomic layer, the two terminations host distinctly different 2DELs.**

Both exhibit Ti 3d<sub>xy</sub>-derived two-dimensional bands, but their Fermi surfaces differ dramatically. The TiO<sub>2</sub> termination displays two sharp concentric rings, corresponding to split electronic bands, whereas the SrO termination exhibits a single ring with preserved degeneracy. Importantly, the two electron liquids have nearly identical band filling and band bending. The distinction therefore cannot simply be attributed to different electron concentrations.

We also investigated the origin of these electronic states. Scanning tunneling spectroscopy establishes that the **TiO<sub>2</sub> termination is metallic immediately after cleavage**, without requiring irradiation-induced oxygen vacancies. In contrast, the **SrO termination initially exhibits semiconducting behavior**. Its transition towards metallicity requires the formation of positively charged oxygen vacancies, together with the reduction of upward band bending under irradiation. This distinction provides a direct explanation for the different formation mechanisms of the two 2DELs.

The pronounced splitting on TiO<sub>2</sub> presents another intriguing result. Our analysis points towards **Zeeman-type rather than conventional Rashba-type splitting**, suggesting a connection between polar distortions of the Ti sublattice and nonrelativistic spin order. The proposed microscopic spin configuration remains to be established by further spin-resolved experiments.

Finally, the exceptional quality of the TiO<sub>2</sub>-derived ARPES spectra allowed us to extract the Eliashberg spectral function and quantify the electron-phonon coupling, obtaining **λ ≈ 0.63**. This provides additional insight into the coupling between electronic and lattice degrees of freedom that may be relevant to controlling spin-dependent phenomena.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the precise atomic termination is not merely a structural detail. **A difference of one atomic layer determines how the 2DEL forms and whether its electronic degeneracy is preserved or lifted.**

The study also illustrates the value of combining real-space atomic-scale microscopy with momentum-resolved spectroscopy. By starting from structurally unambiguous surfaces, we can distinguish intrinsic electronic behavior from effects introduced by uncertain surface preparation.

The full article is available in [*Nature Communications* **16**, 4594 (2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59258-4).
