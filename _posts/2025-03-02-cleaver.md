## How to Cleave Cubic Perovskite Oxides

{% include pdf-figure.html src="/images/posts/srtio3CleaverLego/srtio3CleaverLego.pdf" caption="*An illustration of the cleaving device assembly.*" %}

### For the General Public

Some crystals split easily along well-defined atomic planes. Others, like cubic perovskite oxides, tend to fracture irregularly, making it difficult to obtain clean surfaces for scientific experiments.

In our earlier work, we developed a small device that uses mechanical strain to make strontium titanate (SrTiO<sub>3</sub>) cleave along a specific crystallographic plane, exposing large, atomically flat surfaces.

**But could the same idea work on other materials?**

In our new publication, *"How to cleave cubic perovskite oxides"*, we took a closer look at the method itself. We systematically investigated how to prepare, strain, and cleave SrTiO<sub>3</sub> crystals, identifying the conditions that reliably produce well-defined surfaces.

More importantly, **we successfully applied the same approach to two other perovskites, KTaO<sub>3</sub> and BaTiO<sub>3</sub>**, demonstrating that the method is not unique to SrTiO<sub>3</sub>.

{% include pdf-figure.html src="/images/posts/srtio3CleaverLego/srtio3CleaverOthers.pdf" caption="**One cleaving device, multiple perovskites.** AFM images of cleaved SrTiO<sub>3</sub>, KTaO<sub>3</sub>, and BaTiO<sub>3</sub> surfaces demonstrate that the same strain-assisted cleaving device can successfully produce large, atomically flat regions on different cubic perovskite oxides." %}


The paper is also intended as a practical guide. It includes technical drawings for constructing the device, detailed operating instructions, and examples showing how to recognize a successful cleavage, sometimes even with a simple optical microscope.

I find it particularly satisfying that such a small and relatively simple device can provide access to surfaces that are otherwise remarkably difficult to prepare.

The full study, including instructions for anyone interested in trying the method, is available in [Review of Scientific Instruments](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0233747).



### For Scientists

Preparing truly bulk-terminated (001) surfaces of cubic perovskite oxides remains a challenge in surface science. Their lack of natural cleavage planes typically leads to conchoidal fracture, while conventional surface preparation methods often produce reconstructions or chemically modified terminations.

In our paper, [**"How to cleave cubic perovskite oxides"**](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0233747), published in *Review of Scientific Instruments* **96**, 035113 (2025), we present a detailed and reproducible procedure for strain-assisted cleavage of perovskite single crystals.

Building on our earlier work on SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001), we systematically optimized the cleaving geometry, mechanical pre-straining, and experimental procedure. The paper provides **technical drawings of the device, practical operating instructions, and guidelines for identifying successfully cleaved surfaces**.

A substantial part of the study is dedicated to the morphology of cleaved SrTiO<sub>3</sub>(001), including the distribution of SrO and TiO<sub>2</sub> terminations and the distinction between well-cleaved and conchoidally fractured regions. The latter can often be recognized using optical microscopy alone, while scanning electron microscopy provides further information about the termination domains.

The most important extension is the successful application of the technique to **KTaO<sub>3</sub>(001) and BaTiO<sub>3</sub>(001)**. Together with SrTiO<sub>3</sub>, these results demonstrate the broader applicability of strain-assisted cleavage across cubic perovskite oxides.

Another practical advantage is that each cleavage produces two complementary surfaces, allowing experiments on both sides of the fractured crystal. The compact device can also be adapted to different experimental setups, including ultrahigh-vacuum systems.

Our aim was to make this approach accessible and reproducible for other laboratories, providing a practical route to well-defined perovskite surfaces for fundamental investigations and future applications.

The full methodology is described in [*Review of Scientific Instruments* **96**, 035113 (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0233747).
