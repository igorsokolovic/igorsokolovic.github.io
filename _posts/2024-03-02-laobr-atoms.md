## Seeing Individual Atoms on a Promising Layered Insulator

### For the General Public

Two-dimensional materials have attracted enormous interest for future electronics. Their layers can be stacked to create new devices, but making these devices work requires more than just semiconductors. We also need suitable electrical insulators.

One promising candidate is **lanthanum oxybromide (LaOBr)**, a layered material with a wide electronic bandgap of approximately 5.3 eV and a relatively high dielectric constant of 9. These properties make it attractive for electrically insulating and controlling other two-dimensional materials, while its layered structure allows it to be incorporated into devices through mechanical exfoliation.

In our publication, [*High-κ Wide-Gap Layered Dielectric for Two-Dimensional van der Waals Heterostructures*](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.3c10411), we investigated LaOBr as a promising material for future electronic devices.

My contribution, together with **Saeed Rasouli**, focused on something quite different from device fabrication: **seeing what the surface of LaOBr actually looks like at the atomic scale**.

This presented an interesting experimental challenge. The crystals we worked with were tiny, resembling powder-like crystallites rather than the larger single crystals we typically investigate using noncontact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM). Preparing and examining such small samples in ultrahigh vacuum, while achieving atomic resolution, was far from straightforward.

**But we succeeded!**

After cleaving LaOBr crystals inside our vacuum system, we obtained images resolving the arrangement of individual atoms on their surfaces. The images revealed a **highly ordered square lattice of bromine atoms**, confirming that the crystal cleaves along the weakly bonded gap between its layers.

Importantly, this cleavage produces two equivalent, bromine-terminated surfaces. In other words, the material naturally exposes a well-defined atomic structure when its layers are separated.

For me, the particularly satisfying part was achieving atomic resolution on such unusually small crystals. It was an exciting experimental result in its own right, and it provided direct evidence of the surface structure underlying the material's potential use in two-dimensional electronics.

The broader study also demonstrated that LaOBr can be incorporated into functioning electronic devices, highlighting its promise as a layered dielectric.

Our results were published in [*ACS Nano* **18**, 10397–10406 (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.3c10411).

### For Scientists

The development of van der Waals heterostructures requires dielectric materials that combine a sufficiently large electronic bandgap with effective electrostatic screening and compatibility with the exfoliation and stacking methods used for two-dimensional materials.

**LaOBr is an interesting candidate**, combining a bandgap of approximately 5.3 eV with a measured out-of-plane static dielectric constant of approximately 9. Its layered crystal structure also makes it suitable for mechanical exfoliation and integration into van der Waals heterostructures.

In our collaborative paper, [*High-κ Wide-Gap Layered Dielectric for Two-Dimensional van der Waals Heterostructures*](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.3c10411), published in *ACS Nano*, we investigated the properties and potential applications of this material.

An important part of its characterization was establishing the **atomic structure of the freshly cleaved LaOBr(001) surface**.

Together with **Saeed Rasouli**, I performed atomically resolved noncontact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM) on LaOBr crystals cleaved in ultrahigh vacuum.

What made these experiments particularly demanding was the small, powder-like character of the available crystallites. Obtaining stable atomic-resolution nc-AFM measurements on such small samples is considerably more challenging than working with the larger single crystals commonly used in surface science. To my knowledge, atomic-resolution measurements on crystallites of such powder-like dimensions are exceptionally rare.

Nevertheless, we obtained clear atomic contrast on the cleaved LaOBr(001) surface.

The constant-height nc-AFM images reveal a **square arrangement of surface bromine atoms**, with a lattice parameter of **4.18 ± 0.03 Å**, determined from the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the atomic-resolution images.

The observed surface structure is consistent with cleavage along the van der Waals gap, producing **two mirror-symmetric, Br-terminated surfaces**. This is particularly important because cleavage through other crystallographic planes would expose undercoordinated ions and would not produce the same well-ordered termination.

The measurements therefore provide direct atomic-scale evidence for the expected cleavage mechanism and confirm that LaOBr can expose clean, structurally well-defined surfaces suitable for further investigation.

An additional practical complication was the highly insulating character of LaOBr. Even with a potential difference of 10 V and the tip brought very close to the surface, no measurable tunneling current was detected within the experimental sensitivity. This made force-based microscopy especially valuable for resolving its atomic structure.

While the broader study demonstrated the promising dielectric properties of LaOBr and its successful integration into van der Waals electronic devices, **our nc-AFM contribution established what the material looks like at its very outermost atomic layer**.

For me, this was also a particularly rewarding microscopy challenge. Achieving atomic resolution on such small crystallites, together with Saeed, was an excellent example of how far experimental surface characterization can be pushed beyond the usual sample geometries.

The full study is available in [*ACS Nano* **18**, 10397–10406 (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.3c10411).
