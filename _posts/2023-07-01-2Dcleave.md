Working with small layered materials in ultrahigh vacuum often raises an immediate practical question: **how do you expose a fresh, clean surface suitable for atomic-scale measurements?**

The obvious solution would be to build a dedicated **exfoliation station inside UHV**. That is certainly possible, but it also comes with a price: design work, installation time, testing, and usually a fair amount of laboratory downtime while everything is being integrated and made reliable.

Instead of going down that route, I looked for a **simpler and faster approach**.

The idea was to prepare small cleaving setups outside the chamber in such a way that they could be inserted directly into UHV and used to expose fresh surfaces of layered crystals with minimal effort. In practice, this turned out to be a very effective shortcut. It allowed us to access clean surfaces of a range of layered materials, including several quite new ones, without the need for a complicated dedicated exfoliation system.

What I particularly liked about this approach is that it is **practical, flexible, and fast**. It made it possible to try out materials that would otherwise have required a much larger investment of time and technical work before even knowing whether the resulting surfaces would be interesting.

And, importantly, it worked. The method gave us access to fresh surfaces well suited for **atomic-scale studies** and other UHV-based experiments. In several cases, it opened the door to measurements on materials that were not yet standard samples in surface-science laboratories.

The two photographs below show examples of such **cleaving setups prepared for insertion into UHV**. They may look rather modest, but they turned out to be a very useful way of quickly reaching clean layered surfaces without overcomplicating the experimental workflow.
