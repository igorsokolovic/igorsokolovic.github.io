##Bringing UV Light into a Cryogenic Microscope

I've always been particularly interested in **light–matter interactions**, especially how illumination can change the electronic and chemical properties of surfaces. Studying these effects on different materials requires a reliable way to expose samples to UV light, ideally without disturbing the conditions needed for atomic-resolution measurements.

This becomes quite a challenge when the sample sits inside an ultrahigh-vacuum (UHV) chamber, surrounded by cryogenic shields and just beneath the tip of a noncontact atomic force microscope (nc-AFM).

For a long time, we used a traditional **mercury-vapor UV lamp**. Mercury lamps emit light at several characteristic wavelengths, including prominent UV lines around 254, 313, and 365 nm. Unfortunately, ours frequently overheated, making the irradiation intensity and duration difficult to control. It also produced considerably more optical power than we needed.

I initially considered replacing it with a UV laser. However, more experienced colleagues advised against introducing such a concentrated beam into the cryogenic microscope. Even modest optical heating of the sample and surrounding components could introduce thermal drift, making stable atomic-resolution imaging extremely difficult.

Instead, I decided to **design and build a dedicated UV illumination system** tailored to the microscope and its experimental constraints.

The light source is a **365 nm UV LED**, mounted on a copper cooling rod and powered by a custom electronic circuit I assembled. Operating at **5 V**, it provides approximately **0.1 W of UV optical output**.

With the help of our machine workshop, I developed a housing that mounts onto a UHV flange, with a UV-transparent quartz window allowing light to enter the chamber. The optical path had to be carefully arranged so the beam could pass through a series of openings in the cryogenic shields and reach the sample deep inside the microscope.

Focusing the light presented another challenge. I designed an adjustable optical condenser using two UV-transmitting lenses mounted on sliding rails. Their positions can be adjusted to focus the beam onto the sample, aiming for a spot approximately **1 mm in diameter** at the correct position inside the cryogenic head.

The complete assembly also required a protective enclosure to prevent stray UV radiation from escaping into the laboratory.

Bringing all these elements together involved quite a bit of design, machining, electronics, optical alignment, and troubleshooting. Each part had to work within the limited space and demanding conditions imposed by the cryogenic UHV setup.

**Another advantage is its portability.** The entire lamp assembly is compact and easy to transport, so it hasn't remained confined to its original microscope. It has already accompanied me and several colleagues to experimental beamtimes, where we could use the same UV light source in different setups.

The result is a purpose-built illumination system that offers much better control over UV exposure and makes it possible to investigate light-induced changes on different material surfaces while minimizing disturbances to the microscope.

It's one of those projects I particularly enjoy: a mixture of physics, electronics, optics, and a little help from a very capable machine workshop.
