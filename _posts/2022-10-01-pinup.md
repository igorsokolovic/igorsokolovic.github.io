## Taking responsibility for the MBE growth chamber

Taking responsibility for an experimental laboratory can sometimes mean inheriting an instrument that has seen better days. That was more or less the situation when I took over our **molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) growth chamber**.

The chamber itself is a capable and versatile system, but it had not been used to its full potential for some time. I saw an opportunity to put it to good use and began adapting it to our experimental needs.

The setup has an interesting vertical design, with a sample manipulator that moves between different levels of the chamber, dedicated to **surface analysis, material deposition, and magnetic measurements**. This arrangement offers considerable flexibility, but it also makes maintenance and modifications particularly demanding.

One of the first major tasks involved **removing the entire vertical manipulator**. These components are heavy, precisely engineered, and full of delicate mechanical and vacuum connections. Disassembling such a system is not something to approach casually, especially when a crane is needed just to lift the manipulator out of the chamber!

But modifying the hardware is only part of the job. Once everything is reassembled, the chamber must be brought back to the ultrahigh-vacuum (UHV) conditions required for reliable experiments.

This means a thorough **vacuum bakeout**, during which the entire chamber is heated to drive off water and other molecules adsorbed on its internal surfaces. These contaminants would otherwise slowly desorb during operation, preventing the system from reaching sufficiently low pressures.

For our chamber, this meant building a bakeout enclosure around the instrument and maintaining a temperature of approximately **160 °C for 48 hours**.

And yes, that included the weekend!

With thermocouples monitoring the temperature throughout the process, the challenge was to maintain stable heating while ensuring that the different parts of the system remained within their allowed temperature limits.

Then came the satisfying part: after cooling down, the vacuum gauge showed a pressure of **10<sup>−11</sup> mbar**.

**A successful bakeout, and the chamber was ready for experiments again!**

Taking over this laboratory has involved a considerable amount of hands-on work, from **mechanical maintenance and instrument modifications to vacuum preparation and troubleshooting**. Much of it is time-consuming, requires patience, and happens long before any scientific measurement can begin.

But I find this aspect of experimental physics particularly rewarding. **Understanding an instrument well enough to maintain it, adapt it, and expand what can be done with it is an important achievement in its own right.**

And seeing an underused chamber become a reliable experimental tool again makes all that effort worthwhile.
