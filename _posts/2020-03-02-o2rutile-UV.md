## For the General Public

### How many electrons does an oxygen molecule take when it attaches to a surface?

How many electrons does an oxygen molecule take when it attaches to a surface? On titanium dioxide (TiO<sub>2</sub>), this seemingly simple question has been debated for decades. While theoretical calculations favored molecules carrying two extra electrons (peroxide), earlier experiments suggested that they carried only one (superoxide).

Much of the experimental evidence came from studies using ultraviolet (UV) light to knock oxygen molecules off the surface. By measuring how the rate of desorption changed with light intensity, researchers concluded that removing just one electron was sufficient for a molecule to leave the surface. This was interpreted as evidence for singly charged oxygen.

**Our experiments revealed an important missing piece of the puzzle.**

By imaging exactly the same surface before and after UV irradiation, we discovered that oxygen molecules don't simply leave the surface. Some also **switch between two different adsorption configurations**, while others remain unchanged.

This seemingly small observation changes the interpretation of earlier experiments. It reveals a competing reaction pathway in which electrons can return to the molecules, sometimes leaving them in a different configuration rather than allowing them to desorb.

As a result, even molecules that need to lose two electrons to desorb can exhibit the same experimental behavior previously attributed to singly charged oxygen.

Sometimes, **the key to understanding an experiment is discovering what wasn't being measured**.

## For Scientists

### UV-induced oxygen desorption: why linear kinetics do not necessarily imply superoxo species

One of the strongest experimental arguments for superoxo (O<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>) species on rutile TiO<sub>2</sub>(110) came from UV-induced oxygen desorption. Earlier measurements showed that the desorption rate increased linearly with the concentration of photogenerated holes. This was interpreted as evidence for a one-hole desorption process and, consequently, singly charged adsorbed oxygen.

This was interpreted as evidence for a one-hole desorption process and, consequently, singly charged adsorbed oxygen. A conventional two-hole process would instead be expected to exhibit a quadratic dependence on hole concentration.

However, our **noncontact AFM experiments revealed an additional reaction channel** that challenges this interpretation.

By tracking individual oxygen molecules before and after UV irradiation, we observed that molecules adsorbed on Ti<sub>5c</sub> rows could either desorb or **switch between the π and τ adsorption configurations**. At low UV doses, configuration switching was at least as frequent as desorption.

We propose that both processes involve a common intermediate: a **metastable superoxo state**, formed when an initially peroxo (O<sub>2</sub><sup>2−</sup>) molecule captures a photogenerated hole.

<p style="text-align: center; font-size: 1.15em;">
  π<sup>2−</sup> or τ<sup>2−</sup> + h<sup>+</sup> → O<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>@Ti<sub>5c</sub>
</p>

From this intermediate, two competing reactions are possible:

**Desorption after capturing a second hole:**

<p style="text-align: center; font-size: 1.15em;">
  O<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>@Ti<sub>5c</sub> + h<sup>+</sup> → O<sub>2</sub>(gas)
</p>

**Return to the peroxo state after capturing an electron:**

<p style="text-align: center; font-size: 1.15em;">
  O<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>@Ti<sub>5c</sub> + e<sup>−</sup> → π<sup>2−</sup> or τ<sup>2−</sup>
</p>

The second process can restore the molecule in a different adsorption configuration, explaining the experimentally observed switching.

**The key is that UV irradiation generates both electrons and holes.** If their concentrations increase proportionally, the branching ratio between desorption and readsorption remains approximately constant. Consequently, the overall desorption rate can remain linear in hole concentration:

<p style="text-align: center; font-size: 1.15em;">
  <strong>R<sub>des</sub> ∝ [h<sup>+</sup>]</strong>
</p>

This holds even though the proposed desorption mechanism involves the capture of two holes.

Although an alternative one-hole desorption pathway cannot be entirely excluded, our observations demonstrate that **linear photodesorption kinetics alone cannot establish the initial charge state of adsorbed oxygen**.

These results reconcile earlier UV photodesorption measurements with the energetically preferred peroxo state. The complete experimental results and discussion of the reaction mechanisms can be found in our [PNAS paper](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1922452117).
